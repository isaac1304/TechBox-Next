# Basic cybersecurity for SMBs in Costa Rica

When people talk about cybersecurity, many SMB owners think of sophisticated attacks against banks or governments. The reality is simpler and, for that very reason, more worrying: most incidents at small companies start with an email containing a fake link, a reused password or a backup that was never tested. The good news is that you don’t need a dedicated security team to reduce the risk significantly. In this article I explain five realistic practices, how to apply them in a Costa Rican SMB and what local law says about your customers’ data.

## Why SMBs are an attractive target

Attackers don’t always go after a specific company; they often launch mass campaigns and take advantage of whoever falls for them. SMBs tend to have:

- Accounts shared among several people.
- The same password across several services.
- Devices that are not kept up to date.
- Manual or non-existent backups.
- Little training for the team to recognize scams.

None of these weaknesses requires expensive technology to fix. They require order and decisiveness.

## The five basic practices

### 1. A password manager for the whole team

A password manager lets each person have long, different passwords for every service without memorizing them. It also makes it easy to share access in a controlled way and to remove it when someone leaves the company. It is the change with the best effort-to-impact ratio.

### 2. Two-factor authentication on email, banking and key systems

Two-factor authentication (a code on the phone or a physical key) means a stolen password is not enough to get in. Start with email, because almost every other account can be recovered from it, then move on to online banking and back-office systems.

### 3. Automatic, tested backups

A useful backup meets three conditions: it runs on its own, it is stored outside the office and it is tested by restoring it. Against ransomware, which encrypts files and demands a payment, a tested backup is what lets you recover without negotiating.

### 4. Role-based access

Each person should see only what they need for their job. Reception doesn’t need access to accounting; the sales team doesn’t need administrator rights. When someone leaves the company, their access is closed the same day.

### 5. Training to recognize scams

Most attacks need someone to click, download a file or share a code. Short, practical training with real examples of fake emails and messages greatly reduces that risk. It is worth repeating periodically.

## Illustrative examples

These scenarios are not real customers; they show common situations.

### The "pending invoice" email

Picture a services company in San José that receives an email with a supposed invoice from a well-known supplier. The attachment installs a program that steals passwords. With two-factor authentication enabled, even if the password leaks, the attacker cannot get into the mailbox.

### The bookkeeper’s computer

A shop keeps all its accounting on one person’s computer, with no backup. If that machine breaks or is encrypted by ransomware, the information is lost. An automatic cloud backup, tested once a month, changes that risk completely.

## What the law says in Costa Rica

In Costa Rica, the Law on the Protection of Individuals with regard to the Processing of their Personal Data (Law No. 8968) regulates how personal data is collected, stored and used. It doesn’t only apply to large companies: if your SMB stores customers’ names, ID numbers, phone numbers or emails, it concerns you. The Agency for the Protection of Inhabitants’ Data (PRODHAB) is the authority in charge.

Some practical implications:

- Ask for consent to use the data and explain what it is used for.
- Protect the data with reasonable security measures.
- Allow people to see, correct or request deletion of their data.

## Review your vendors and tools too

Your SMB’s security also depends on third parties: the invoicing system vendor, whoever manages your website, the company that provides your IT support. Some questions worth asking them:

- Who has access to our data, and with what permissions?
- Do they use two-factor authentication to access our systems?
- How often do they back up the information, and where is it stored?
- What do they do, and how quickly do they notify us, if they detect an incident?
- What happens to our data if we stop working with them?

It is also worth checking "orphan" accounts: services someone signed up for with a personal email, domains registered to a former employee, or free tools where customer documents were uploaded. A simple list of tools, with an owner and the way each is accessed, is already a big step forward.

## Warning signs you shouldn’t ignore

- Emails asking you to change a supplier’s bank account.
- Sign-in alerts from unknown locations.
- Computers that suddenly run much slower, or files with strange extensions.
- Customers reporting odd messages coming from your email.

If you see any of these signs, the best move is to stop, verify through another channel and get help before going further.

## A 30-day plan to get started

- **Week 1:** enable two-factor authentication on every email account and on banking.
- **Week 2:** roll out a password manager and change shared passwords.
- **Week 3:** set up automatic backups and do a first restore test.
- **Week 4:** review role-based access, close accounts of people who have left and run a first training session.

## Common mistakes

- Believing "it won’t happen to us because we’re small".
- Buying tools before sorting out access and backups.
- Having backups that have never been restored.
- Leaving former employees’ accounts active.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an expensive antivirus?

Current operating systems include reasonable basic protection if kept up to date. Before buying more software, make sure two-factor authentication, passwords, backups and access are covered.

### What should I do if I think I’ve been attacked?

Disconnect the affected device from the network, change passwords from another device, notify your bank if accounts were accessed and get technical help. Document what happened.

### How often should I test backups?

We recommend at least a periodic restore test, and always after a major change to your systems.

### Does Law 8968 apply to my SMB?

If you store personal data about customers, suppliers or staff, it probably does. Consult a lawyer about your specific case.
